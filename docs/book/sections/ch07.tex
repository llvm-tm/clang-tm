\chapter[The Semantics of Transactions]{The Semantics of Transactions:
Isolation Levels and Their Cost}
\label{chap:isolation}
\index{isolation level}\index{snapshot isolation}\index{serializability!cost of}
\index{anomaly}\index{write skew}\index{phantom}

Part~III closed with the strongest conditions in the book: serializability,
strict serializability, opacity. Strength is not free, and it is not the only
direction the space has. Databases spent fifty years exploring what lies
\emph{below} serializability, built a named taxonomy of the rungs and the
anomalies each admits, and developed a working cost theory for the trade. That
theory is the subject of this chapter. It matters to a TM author for three
reasons. First, it explains why every backend in Part~IV sits at the top of
the ladder: the weaker rungs are \emph{structurally} unsound in shared memory
in a way they are not in a database. Second, the mechanisms invented to
implement the weaker rungs --- multi-version snapshots, early lock release,
snapshot thresholds --- are the same mechanisms our backends use while
guaranteeing the strongest level, and understanding which part of a mechanism
carries semantics and which part is mere engineering is the difference between
tuning a backend and breaking one. Third, when TM meets storage
(Chapter~19), the two worlds collide on exactly this ground: TiKV and
Percolator speak snapshot isolation, Calvin speaks static transactions, and
the reader needs one vocabulary to place them all.

This chapter uses the history vocabulary of \S\ref{sec:histories-legality-opacity}
throughout: histories, committed projections, read-from sources, real-time
order. Where Chapter~5 built the ladder upward from serializability, this
chapter descends from it, and then shows where the ladder stops applying.

\section{The Shape of the Space: Dimensions of Transaction Semantics}
\label{sec:iso-dimensions}
\index{transaction semantics!dimensions of}

A transaction semantics is not a point chosen from a menu; it is a position in
a product of independent questions. Ask them in order and the design space
becomes legible --- for databases, for TM, and for the hybrids of
Chapter~19. Table~\ref{tab:iso-dimensions} lists the dimensions that carry
real weight, with the range databases chose and the range TM is forced
into. The forcing is systematic: in a database the observer of a transaction
is a \emph{client behind a protocol boundary} --- a separate process whose
every view is a request the engine answers from committed state --- while in
TM the observer is a \emph{peer inside the address space}, whose every load
hits the same cells the runtime speculates on. Read the two rightmost columns
against that one difference and they stop looking like separate traditions
and start looking like one design space probed at different boundaries.

\subsection*{The taxonomic lineage}

The vocabulary above is inherited, and the inheritance is contested in useful
ways. Gray's textbook \cite{gray93tp} organizes semantics by \emph{strictness}
--- who may see what before whom, in what commit order --- yielding the
serial-and-strict hierarchy that Chapter~5 restated as real-time-preserving
serializations. ANSI SQL organized by \emph{level labels} (READ UNCOMMITTED
through SERIALIZABLE defined by permitted phenomena), which failed:
Berenson and colleagues \cite{berenson95critique} showed in 1995 that the
ANSI definitions neither capture all anomalies (write skew and lost update
were missing from the matrix) nor order the levels correctly, and proposed
defining levels by the anomalies they admit. Adya generalized both into
\emph{weak consistency theory}: each level is a class of histories, and one
level is stronger than another when its class contains the other's
\cite{adya99thesis} --- the containment picture drawn as
Figure~\ref{fig:iso-ladder}. Kleppmann's engineering survey \cite{kleppmann17}
is the modern reference for what production systems actually promise at each
rung. This book follows Berenson's anomaly-first method and Adya's containment
language, because both translate directly into the history vocabulary of
Chapter~5 and into TLA+ invariants in Chapter~6; and it follows Gray in
treating commit order as a dimension of its own, because the backends of
Part~IV differ along it visibly (single lock, global clock, commit chain).

Figure~\ref{fig:db-tx-landscape} shows the database side of the boundary that
drives everything else: why one of the hardest problems in transaction
semantics --- keeping a doomed transaction from corrupting its observers ---
is answered for free by an interface, at zero runtime cost.

\section{The Isolation Ladder}
\label{sec:iso-ladder}
\index{isolation level!read uncommitted}\index{isolation level!read committed}
\index{isolation level!repeatable read}\index{isolation level!snapshot isolation}
\index{serializable snapshot isolation}

Restated in history vocabulary, the classic ladder has six rungs. Read each
bullet as a constraint on the \emph{read-from} source of \S\ref{sec:histories-legality-opacity}:
which write a read event may legitimately draw its value from.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Read uncommitted (RU).} A read may take its value from an
uncommitted write. Dirty reads are legal; so, trivially, is almost everything
else. No mainstream shared-memory language offers an analogue, and
\S\ref{sec:iso-not-in-tm} explains why the analogue would be a segfault menu.

\item \textbf{Read committed (RC).} Every read takes its value from a
committed write --- but different reads within one transaction may take
different snapshots. \emph{Non-repeatable reads} are legal: $r_T(x){=}1$ and
a later $r_T(x){=}2$ after some $T'$ committed in between. This is the default
in most production databases, which is itself a fact worth arguing about
(\S\ref{sec:iso-cost}).

\item \textbf{Repeatable read (RR).} All reads within a transaction see one
consistent snapshot, so re-reads do not change; but new objects may appear
between two reads of a \emph{predicate} --- the \emph{phantom} anomaly.
Row-level implementations pin the rows they touched, not the range.

\item \textbf{Snapshot isolation (SI).} Each transaction reads from a single
logical snapshot (an instant in the committed history), and a write commits
only if no concurrent transaction wrote the same object (\emph{first
committer wins}, a WW test). Phantoms are structurally impossible: the
snapshot is a consistent whole database. What SI still admits is the
infamous \emph{write skew} (\S\ref{sec:iso-anomalies}). Formally: the
committed projection admits a serialization \emph{whose legality is checked
only against snapshot reads and WW conflicts} --- the weak validation that
makes it fast.

\item \textbf{Serializable snapshot isolation (SSI).} SI plus a runtime check
that rejects the anti-dependency cycles responsible for write skew and its
relatives. It is ``SI with a proof obligation attached'': reads remain
snapshot-cheap, and the price moves to cycle bookkeeping at commit
(\cite{kleppmann17}; the mechanism is described in
\S\ref{sec:iso-cost} and checked in \S\ref{sec:iso-worked-example}).

\item \textbf{Serializability (S),} and above it \textbf{strict
serializability.} The rungs of Chapter~5. Every anomaly in the Berenson
matrix disappears, and with it the need for the application to reason about
other transactions at all.
\end{itemize}

The containment reading (Adya): each rung's set of admissible histories is a
proper superset of the rung below's, so every guarantee you drop is a hole
some implementation can drive an anomaly through --- and each hole has a name
and a witness, next section.

The witnesses, compactly, over accounts \texttt{x=y=100} with the invariant
\texttt{x+y $\geq$ 200}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Dirty read.} $w_{T_1}(x){\leftarrow}0$; $r_{T_2}(x){\to}0$;
$\mathrm{abort}(T_1)$. $T_2$ acted on money that never existed. Only RU
permits it; this is the row that RU exists to label.

\item \emph{Lost update.} $r_{T_1}(x){\to}0$; $r_{T_2}(x){\to}0$;
$w_{T_1}(x){\leftarrow}1$; $w_{T_2}(x){\leftarrow}1$. Both increments, one
result. RC permits it because its reads are only committed, not stable;
everything from RR on detects the WW conflict.

\item \emph{Phantom.} $r_{T_1}(\texttt{SUM over }[1..9]){\to}9$; $T_2$
inserts row 10 and commits; $r_{T_1}(\texttt{SUM}){\to}10$. Row-stable
levels cannot stop it because $T_1$'s second read touched a \emph{predicate},
not a row; snapshot levels kill it structurally.

\item \emph{Write skew.} Two doctors on call; invariant: at least one stays.
$T_1$: reads \texttt{mine=off, other=on}, decides both may rest, writes
\texttt{mine=rest}. $T_2$ mirror-image. Each reads before the other's write;
neither write touches the object the other read; both pass WW validation.
Commit both: \texttt{mine=rest, other=rest} --- an outcome no serial
execution produces. The two transactions have \emph{no write-write conflict
and no read-write conflict with a stable value}; what they have is an
\emph{anti-dependency}: $T_1$ read a value $T_2$ later overwrote. Formally,
$T_1 \mathop{\sim} T_2$ when $T_1$ reads-from a value that $T_2$ writes
afterward. SI executions are serializable exactly when the graph of read,
write, and anti-dependencies is acyclic \cite{berenson95critique}; SI permits
the cycle, SSI's commit-time cycle detection does not. This is stated for
use, not proved --- it is the sort of theorem to take as a paper exercise.
\end{itemize}

The historical footnote matters for how to read the matrix: ANSI defined its
levels \emph{syntactically}, by which phenomena a standard-conforming
implementation might exhibit, and the critique \cite{berenson95critique}
showed (a) the phenomenon list was incomplete --- lost update and write skew
were absent from the ANSI definitions --- and (b) no level between RR and S
could ever have prevented them anyway. The lesson a backend author should
take is methodological: define your guarantees by the anomalies you can
demonstrate against them, not by the label. Chapter~6's bug-injection
workflow (\S\ref{sec:verify-workflow}) is exactly that method, mechanized.

\section{The Price Each Rung Buys}
\label{sec:iso-cost}
\index{abort probability!derivation from isolation}\index{wasted work}

Weak levels are not free; they are \emph{cheap to validate and expensive to
live with}. This section quantifies the cheap side, continuing the geometric
retry model of \S\ref{sec:math-example}: expected attempts
$E=1/(1-p_{\mathrm{abort}})$, effective service time
$S_{\mathrm{eff}} = S/(1-p_{\mathrm{abort}})$ feeding Little's Law. What
Chapter~5 left as a parameter --- the abort probability $p$ --- isolation
semantics actually derives.

Assume a hot set of $k$ objects, each transaction reading $r$ and writing $w$
of them, and $C{-}1$ concurrent contenders, accesses uniform and independent
(the approximation regime of \S\ref{sec:math-example} and \S5.10's two-phase
validation cost; both fail together when access is data-dependent,
and the escape hatch is the same: model checking, Part~III). A commit attempt
fails validation iff some contender conflicts on what the level checks:

\begin{align}
p_{\mathrm{abort}}^{\mathrm{SI}} &\approx 1 - \left(1 - \tfrac{w}{k}\right)^{w(C-1)}
\qquad\text{(WW test only; reads never abort)}\\[1mm]
p_{\mathrm{abort}}^{\mathrm{S}} &\approx 1 - \left(1 - \tfrac{w}{k}\right)^{(2r+w)(C-1)}
\qquad\text{(RW, WR, and WW; value-validated OCC)}
\end{align}

The exponent difference is the whole story of SI: a serializable optimistic
transaction's validation is violated by $2r+w$ object-chances per contender;
an SI transaction's by $w$. Reads --- typically the bulk of a transaction ---
drop out of the failure condition entirely. Table~\ref{tab:iso-cost} gives
numbers for a bank-like workload ($k=50$ hot accounts, $r=4$, $w=1$);
Figure~\ref{fig:iso-cost} draws them.

Three honest caveats, each of which is a paragraph in \S5.10: (i) the
independence approximation overstates conflicts for skewed-but-local
workloads and understates them for global hot rows; (ii) serializable here
means value-validated OCC --- a two-phase-locking serializable engine trades
the retry multiplier for waits, and its ``attempts'' stay at $1.0$ while its
throughput collapses elsewhere in the queue; (iii) SSI pays a third term
again: snapshot reads with commit-time anti-dependency tracking cost
roughly $1.1$--$1.4\times$ SI in reported measurements of real engines
(\cite{kleppmann17}, FoundationDB and CockroachDB engineering posts). The
row a database designer walks is SI $\to$ SSI $\to$ S, and the walk is made
because the anomaly row of Table~\ref{tab:anomaly-matrix} is a
\emph{correctness} row, not a performance one.

Read the table once more from the TM side: our backends all pay the first
row's cost and consider it a bargain, because the second row's saving is
bought with the only anomaly in shared memory that turns into the crash class
of \S\ref{sec:crash-without-opacity}: state that a consumer can act on. In a
database, write skew costs a double-dispatched doctor; in a bank simulator it
costs a negative reserve; both are recoverable. A pointer torn the same way
is not.

\section{From Mechanism to Semantics and Back}
\label{sec:iso-mechanisms}
\index{write-back}\index{write-through}\index{early lock release}
\index{version management!multi-version}\index{static transactions}

Semantics and mechanism are often conflated in the backend literature, and
the conflation costs students chapters of confusion. The clean way to separate
them: the dimensions of Table~\ref{tab:iso-dimensions} are \emph{promises};
the mechanism knobs below are \emph{machines} that can implement various
promises at various costs. Two machines can share a knob and guarantee
different things; one machine can be tuned across several promises. This
section lists the knobs and who turns them; Part~IV is the catalogue.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Version management: overwrite vs version.} Single-version
designs mutate the object and undo on abort (SGL, TL2); multi-version designs
install new versions and let readers pick by snapshot (MVLOG; JVSTM and OFG
downstream). MVCC is the mechanism by which databases \emph{implement} the SI
promise of stable snapshot reads --- but note the decoupling: multi-version
TM backends (Chapter~14) use versioning for \emph{concurrency under
serializability}, validating read-sets that single-version designs do not.
The snapshot mechanism is reusable; the SI semantics is a separate promise
about what need not be validated.

\item \textbf{Write policy: write-back vs write-through, and TinySTM's three.}
Write-back buffers speculative writes until commit (sandboxed);
write-through publishes immediately (eager). TinySTM \cite{felber08tinystm}
exposes the middle ground as configurations: \texttt{WBCTL}, writes buffered
but locks released early (contention-triggered); \texttt{WBETL}, locks
released eagerly for writes already owned; \texttt{WT}, eager publishing with
undo on abort. \emph{Early lock release} is the pivotal trick: it preserves
serializability because a released lock's value remains protected by a
per-object version counter checked at commit --- the promise (serializable)
survives while the machine (lock holding) shrinks. Remove the version check,
keep the early release, and you have EAGER\_BROKEN: the same machine, a
different and worse promise. That single missing check is
\S\ref{sec:crash-without-opacity}'s segfault.

\item \textbf{Conflict detection: eager vs lazy.} Pessimistic systems detect
at first access (lock acquisition); optimistic at commit (validation).
Optimistic plus sandboxed writes is the OCC pattern of NOrec and TL2
(Chapter~10); the family matrix Table~\ref{tab:family-matrix} and the snapshot
taxonomy Table~\ref{tab:snapshot-taxonomy} organize Part~IV along exactly
this knob.

\item \textbf{Static vs dynamic transactions.} A static transaction's read
and write sets are known before it runs; Calvin \cite{thomson12calvin} makes
a database of them: control decisions are deferred until the full batch of
intentions is known, transactions are \emph{serialized first and executed
deterministically afterwards}, and validation cost becomes scheduling cost.
The TM analogue is SGL (Chapter~9): with one lock, every interleaving is
serializable by construction, and the ``schedule'' is lock order. Calvin's
lesson is the ladder's deepest cost saving --- anomalies are cheap to prevent
\emph{if you know the whole future}; the GPU systems of Chapter~18
rediscover this per-warp.

\item \textbf{Snapshot thresholds.} GPU TMs batch loads into snapshot reads
(Chapter~18, \S\ref{sec:snapshot-example}; \cite{chen17snapshot}): reads
inside the batch are answered from one instant, like SI, while commit
validation stays serializable. It is the cleanest existing demonstration
that the \emph{mechanism} of snapshot reads and the \emph{semantics} of SI
come apart: the GPU gets SI-style read stability and still rejects every
anomaly in Table~\ref{tab:anomaly-matrix}.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Half That Cannot Cross Over}
\label{sec:iso-not-in-tm}
\index{isolation level!not transferable to TM}\index{cliff argument}

Here is the chapter's thesis, stated plainly: the isolation ladder has a
cliff, and shared memory is below it. Not ``weak levels are inadvisable'' in
shared memory --- they are unsound defaults whose failure mode is the program
itself. Five observations, cumulatively.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The boundary is the guarantee.} A database can offer dirty
reads because the reader is a requestor: even the dirtiest answer is a
\emph{copy} handed across a protocol boundary at one instant, and the
application's stack is built by the application, never by the engine. Shared
memory has no such boundary: weakening visibility hands the peer a live
location mid-mutation. The crash-awareness dimension of
Table~\ref{tab:iso-dimensions} is free on one side of the boundary and
structural on the other. \S\ref{sec:crash-without-opacity} is the executable
proof.

\item \textbf{Applications cannot reconcile pointers.} The weak-level bargain
in databases is ``the anomaly is visible, and your application logic absorbs
it'' --- re-validate before dispatch, use idempotent operations, let the
billing job fix double-debits. Real applications do exactly this, which is
why RC is a production default. The reconciliation assumes anomalies are
\emph{values you can re-read and re-decide about}. A consumer that latched
\texttt{count=1} cannot re-decide its way out of dereferencing
\texttt{inventory[0]}; pointer-and-length invariants have no idempotent
repair. Every weak level's escape hatch is an application discipline that
shared-memory code does not have.

\item \textbf{The concurrency prize is smaller than it looks.} Databases pay
for strength in \emph{waits}: locks held across interactive workloads stall
throughput, and that stall is the reason weak levels exist. TM aborts instead
of waiting: our serializable OCC designs already run fully concurrently
inside transactions and pay only the retry multiplier of
\S\ref{sec:iso-cost}. The prize weak levels sell --- not waiting for
uncommitted writers --- is something serializable TM never withholds. You
cannot sell a saving on a cost that isn't there.

\item \textbf{Predicate structure does not exist.} RR-vs-SI separation turns
entirely on range predicates over an index. An address space is not a
predicate-indexed collection; ``phantom'' over memory means ``an object
appeared,'' which in a GC-less program is precisely the lost-update/aliasing
family the top rung already forbids. The rung's reason for being fails to
transfer.

\item \textbf{Recoverability collapses.} The Gray--Reuter levels of
recoverability --- cascading aborts, strict schedules
\cite{gray93tp} --- exist because committed-but-uncommitted chains cross
processes and logs. In TM, undo is a private log in a runtime that never
exposes a partially committed transaction to anyone; there is no outside for
a cascade to escape into. The dimension does not weaken the top rung; it is
simply absent.
\end{enumerate}

What does cross over is the machinery: MVCC for read stability, early lock
release, snapshot batching, static scheduling. The backends of Part~IV borrow
all of it. What does not cross over are the promises below serializability,
and the reason is one sentence the reader should be able to quote in an exam:
\emph{a database's isolation levels are guarantees about the answers it gives
to clients; a TM's correctness conditions are guarantees about the state its
peers can act on --- and only the top rung bounds the second.}

\section{Worked Example: Write Skew, Checked}
\label{sec:iso-worked-example}
\index{write skew!model check of}\index{snapshot isolation!model check of}

The witnesses of \S\ref{sec:iso-anomalies} were hand histories; this section
replays one under TLC, so the reader can see the same argument in the
machine. The scenario is the doctor pair restated as a bank: accounts
\texttt{x=y=500}, a per-account reserve floor \texttt{RESERVE=500}
implemented as a cross-check --- each transaction reads both balances and
draws \texttt{DRAW=400} from its own account only if the \emph{other} account
can absorb the shortfall, a policy whose only enforcement is the reads.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The write-skew history at SI (both transactions
commit; the outcome admits no serial explanation).},
label={lst:banksi-history}]
T1: r(x)=500   r(y)=500   w(x<-100)   commit
T2: r(x)=500   r(y)=500   w(y<-100)   commit
final: x=100, y=100   -- both drew; neither saw the other's draw.
\end{lstlisting}

Trace \texttt{lst:banksi-history} against the ladder: no read sees an
uncommitted write (each reads 500, committed), the snapshot of each is
internally consistent, no WW conflict exists (disjoint write sets) --- every
condition SI checks passes. And the final state violates the invariant, so
no serialization of \{T1, T2\} explains it: the history is SI and not
serializable. The dependency graph has $T_1 \sim T_2$ (T1 read $y$, T2 wrote
$y$ later) and $T_2 \sim T_1$ --- a 2-cycle of anti-dependencies, the
smallest SSI-rejectable configuration.

The machine version is \rep{docs/proofs/BankSI.tla}, one model with a boolean
constant \texttt{SERIALIZABLE}:

\begin{verbatim}
    make -C docs/proofs check-teaching
\end{verbatim}

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{BankSI-skew.cfg} (\texttt{SERIALIZABLE=FALSE} --- validation
checks only the WW rule): TLC returns \emph{Invariant Reserve is violated}
with the trace of Listing~\ref{lst:banksi-history}, one commit after the
other, at model depth 6.
\item \texttt{BankSI-serial.cfg} (\texttt{SERIALIZABLE=TRUE} --- validation
adds the read-set check against later writes): no error. The extra check is
exactly the term $(2r+w)$ vs $w$ from \S\ref{sec:iso-cost}: the model shows
the anomaly, and the same model with the serializable validation installed
shows the hole closed.
\end{itemize}

The point of re-running this here is the correspondence, three ways: the
history notation of Chapter~5, the anomaly row of Table~\ref{tab:anomaly-matrix},
and the TLC verdict all say the same sentence about the same six events.
Exercises extend the model: widen \texttt{DRAW} until SI's two-phase
validation also rejects (find the boundary), and add the SSI cycle rule as a
third configuration --- reject a commit whose anti-dependency edges close a
cycle among committed transactions.

\section{Summary}

Transaction semantics is a product of dimensions --- visibility, strictness,
ordering, anomaly set, crash-awareness, recoverability, progress, durability
--- and the two traditions explored different faces of it because their
observers sit at different places: a database client behind a protocol
boundary, a TM thread inside the address space. The database ladder
(RU $\subset$ RC $\subset$ RR $\subset$ SI $\subset$ SSI $\subset$ S) is
anomaly-defined after Berenson \cite{berenson95critique}, containment-ordered
after Adya \cite{adya99thesis}, and costs what \S\ref{sec:iso-cost} computes:
weaker levels validate $w$ instead of $2r+w$ object-chances per contender and
buy their concurrency by admitting named holes. Every rung below
serializability is one of those holes; in shared memory the holes are
crash-awareness failures, so the only sound rung is the top, and the sound
TM design is the one that borrows weak-level \emph{machinery} --- MVCC, early
lock release, snapshot batching, static scheduling --- while keeping the
strong \emph{promise}. The mechanisms are in Part~IV; the promises were here.

\begin{enumerate}
